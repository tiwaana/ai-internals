Label six or seven colleges, then hit learn. Nobody sets the weights by hand in a real model, and you do not have to either. Minsky and Papert, 1969. They proved this cannot be done, correctly, and the field took it as a verdict on the whole idea. Add one hidden layer and the region stops being a straight cut, and the four points separate easily. Everybody knew that in 1969 too. 📚 Feed the Parrot — you are the training data

This really is a language model. Its "weights" are counts of which word follows which, learned from the text below. Edit the text → the model changes. Nothing is hardcoded.

Training data — edit me, then retrain

Try this: type the cat and sample a few times — it parrots the text. Now add a line to the training data like "the cat drove a tractor to Denver.", hit Retrain, and sample again: a brand-new possibility appears with a real probability next to it. That is the whole loop — data → weights → probabilities → a sampled word. Temperature doesn't change what the model knows; it only changes how boldly it picks. A real LLM is this, with billions of weights and a much longer memory of the context.

🔦 Spotlight — build an attention head by hand

The word "it" has to figure out what it refers to. It does that by shining a spotlight over the other words. You control what the spotlight is looking for; the strength on each word is a real dot product, softmaxed live.

What just happened: each word carries a small vector of features (its key). Your three sliders are the query. Attention strength = query · key ÷ √d, pushed through a softmax so the numbers add to 100%. The answer is a blend of every word's value, weighted by that percentage — attention never picks one word, it mixes. Turn focus down and the blend goes mushy; turn it up and it snaps to a single word. And the punchline: in a real transformer nobody sets those sliders by hand — training discovers them. ⛷️ Downhill — real gradient descent, two weights

A model with exactly 2 weights: guess = w × coffees + b. Left = the data and the model's current guess. Right = the loss landscape: every possible (w, b), coloured by how wrong it is. Training = roll the ball downhill. Drag the ball. Click the left plot to add data.

What you're learning: the yellow arrow is the real gradient — the compass needle pointing straight uphill. A training step walks the opposite way, and the size of that walk is the learning rate. Crank it past about 0.20 and watch the ball fling itself across the valley and explode: that is a genuine divergence, the single most common way real training runs die. Drag the ball somewhere terrible and hit Run to watch it find its way home. train_hello.py does exactly this — same loop, same arithmetic — just with hundreds of weights instead of two, in a landscape nobody can draw.
